TOPIC GROUP 01 · GRAPHS & GRADIENTS

Graphs and Gradients

Exam task

Given a scatter graph with a line of best fit, you must be able to judge whether the line is appropriate, calculate its gradient from two points, state the gradient's units, and describe the gradient's shape (positive/negative/zero; constant/increasing/decreasing). Nearly every motion question in this unit builds on this skill.

Scatter graphs and the line of best fit

Physics measurements are never perfect — every point carries experimental uncertainty. A line of best fit is a single straight line or smooth curve drawn through the pattern of points so that roughly equal numbers of points lie above and below it, and it follows the overall trend rather than any single outlier.

  • The line of best fit may be a straight line or a curve — the shape must match the pattern of the data, not your preference.
  • A point that lies far away from the pattern of all other points is an outlier. Do not force the line through it; flag it instead.
  • When calculating a gradient, always pick two points that lie on the line (ideally far apart), never two measured data points.

Command terms

Annotate = add brief notes to a diagram or graph. Sketch = draw the general shape with relevant features labelled. Plot = mark the exact positions of points. Examiners award marks for labelled axes, units on both axes, and a stated gradient value with units.

What a gradient actually is

The gradient of a straight line tells you how much the y-value changes for every 1-unit increase in the x-value. It is a ratio — "rise over run":

Gradient = rise ÷ run, taken between two points on the line

Choose two points that sit exactly on the line of best fit. Mark their coordinates, subtract, divide. A larger separation between the points gives a more reliable gradient because reading errors matter less.

Property of the gradientWhat it looks like on the graphWhat it means (when y = position)
PositiveLine rises left → rightObject moves in the positive direction
NegativeLine falls left → rightObject moves in the negative direction (back towards the start)
ZeroHorizontal linePosition never changes — the object is at rest
ConstantStraight lineRate of change is steady (constant velocity)
IncreasingCurve getting steeperVelocity is increasing (acceleration)
DecreasingCurve getting flatterVelocity is decreasing (slowing down)

Units of the gradient

The gradient's units are always "y units divided by x units" — they are not optional and they are not the same as the axis units separately.

  • Position–time graph → gradient units = m/s → the gradient is a velocity.
  • Velocity–time graph → gradient units = (m/s)/s = m/s² → the gradient is an acceleration.
  • A distance–mass graph would give units of m/kg — often a physically meaningless quantity, which is itself an evaluation point: not every gradient has physical meaning.
Worked example — gradient of a distance–time graph

Task: A cyclist travels 240 m between t = 10 s and t = 40 s on a straight road. The line of best fit on the distance–time graph is straight. Calculate the gradient and state what it represents.

Step 1 — identify the two points on the line: (10 s, 60 m) and (40 s, 300 m). (The 240 m travelled equals 300 − 60 — gradient uses the change, not the reading.)

Step 2 — divide: m = (300 − 60) / (40 − 10) = 240 / 30 = 8.0 m/s.

Step 3 — units and meaning: the y-axis is metres and the x-axis is seconds, so the gradient units are m/s. A straight line means the gradient is constant, so the cyclist travels at a constant speed of 8.0 m/s.

Interactive set · Self-check 1

A position–time graph for an object is a horizontal line at x = 15 m. What does the gradient tell you?

A. The object is moving at 15 m/s
B. The object has travelled 15 m in total
C. The object is stationary — its position is not changing
D. The object is accelerating at 15 m/s²
A horizontal line has zero gradient: zero change in position per second means the object stays at x = 15 m. Students who pick A read the axis value (15) as a speed — the gradient, not the height, carries the velocity.
Interactive set · Self-check 2

The line on a position–time graph curves and gets steeper as time increases. The object's velocity is:

A. Constant and positive
B. Increasing (positive acceleration)
C. Decreasing (slowing down)
D. Zero, because the graph is curved
Steeper gradient → larger velocity. A steadily steepening curve means the velocity grows each second: positive acceleration. Curved does NOT mean random — the shape of the curve tells you exactly how the velocity changes.

Do not confuse

Gradient vs value. On any graph, the height of a point is the y-value itself (e.g. the position); the steepness is the gradient (e.g. the velocity). A question that asks "what is the object's velocity at t = 5 s?" wants the gradient of the position–time graph at that point — never the position reading.

TOPIC GROUP 02 · DESCRIBING MOTION IN 1D

Motion Variables: Position, Velocity, Acceleration

Start with a number line

Every 1-D motion problem begins by building a number line for reference: choose a point to be zero (the origin), then choose a positive direction. Without these two decisions, "+4 m/s" and "−4 m/s" are meaningless.

A number line fixes the origin and the positive direction

The object above sits 20 m on the negative side of the origin. If it later moves to +10 m, its displacement is +30 m — final minus initial, regardless of the path taken.

The three variables

Position (x)Where the object is on the number line at a given time. Units: metres (m). May be positive, negative or zero.
Velocity (v)The rate of change of position — speed and direction. Units: m/s. Sign gives direction: + is along the positive axis, − the opposite way.
Acceleration (a)The rate of change of velocity. Units: m/s². Positive acceleration means the velocity number increases (note: this may still mean slowing down! — see the rule below).
ScalarVector
DefinitionSize onlySize and direction
Motion examplesDistance, speed, mass, time, energyDisplacement, velocity, acceleration, force
Sign meaningA negative sign is (almost) meaninglessThe sign is the direction along your number line

"Change in" means final − initial

The symbol Δ (delta) always means final value − initial value. So Δx = xfinal − xinitial is displacement, and Δv = vfinal − vinitial is the change in velocity. An object that speeds up from +2 m/s to +8 m/s has Δv = +6 m/s; one that slows from −8 m/s to −2 m/s has Δv = +6 m/s as well (it became more positive).

The speeding-up / slowing-down rule — learn this one cold

An object speeds up when velocity and acceleration have the same sign (same direction → the velocity grows away from zero). It slows down when they have opposite signs (the velocity is being pushed back towards zero). "Positive acceleration" does NOT automatically mean "getting faster".

CaseVelocity signAcceleration signResult
A++Speeding up, moving in + direction
B−−Speeding up, moving in − direction
C+−Slowing down while still moving + (about to turn around)
D−+Slowing down while moving − (about to turn around)
Worked example — deciding speeding up or slowing down

Task: A Stockholm T-bana train leaves a station heading east (taken as positive). Its velocity changes from 0 to +14 m/s in 20 s. Later it brakes from +14 m/s to 0 in 28 s. Describe the acceleration in each phase.

Phase 1 (leaving the station): a = Δv / Δt = (14 − 0) / 20 = +0.70 m/s². Velocity + and acceleration + → same sign → speeding up.

Phase 2 (braking): a = (0 − 14) / 28 = −0.50 m/s². Velocity still + but acceleration − → opposite signs → slowing down. The acceleration is negative even though the train never reverses — it simply arrives at the next station and stops.

Interactive set · Self-check 3

A car moves along a number line with velocity −10 m/s and acceleration −2 m/s². It is:

A. Slowing down in the negative direction
B. Slowing down in the positive direction
C. Speeding up in the negative direction
D. At rest, because both values are negative
Same sign (both −) → the velocity grows away from zero: −10, −12, −14… The car speeds up heading in the negative direction. Negatives never mean "stopped" — they mean "opposite to the positive direction you chose".
Interactive set · Self-check 4

A skateboarder rolls west at 6 m/s and gradually comes to a stop. Taking east as positive, the acceleration is:

A. Positive, because the velocity magnitude decreases towards zero
B. Negative, because the skateboarder is moving west
C. Zero, because the skateboarder never turns around
D. Impossible to determine without the mass
West = negative velocity (−6 m/s). Slowing down means a is opposite to v, so a is positive. Mass is irrelevant to acceleration signs — that is a forces question (Newton's 2nd law), not a kinematics one.

Do not confuse

Distance vs displacement. Distance is a scalar path length (always ≥ 0, counts every metre walked). Displacement is a vector: final position minus initial position (may be zero even after a long walk, e.g. returning to the start). A lap of a 400 m running track gives distance = 400 m but displacement = 0.

TOPIC GROUP 03 · MOTION GRAPHS

Position–Time and Velocity–Time Graphs

Two graphs carry almost all of M10 kinematics. The single most valuable sentence in this unit is: "As the slope goes, so goes the velocity" on a position–time graph; on a velocity–time graph, the slope is the acceleration and the area is the displacement."

Position–time graphs: the slope is the velocity

Position–time shapes

Horizontal = at rest. Straight diagonal = constant velocity. Curve flattening = slowing down; curve steepening = speeding up.

Reading a velocity from the slope

Rise 50 m over a run of 10 s → v = 5 m/s. The same triangle drawn anywhere on a straight line gives the same answer: constant velocity.

Velocity–time graphs: slope is acceleration, area is displacement

Velocity–time graph — two readings from one line

Ramp up 0–4 s (accelerating), constant 15 m/s from 4–8 s, ramp down 8–14 s (decelerating back to rest). Slope of the first segment: a = (15 − 0)/(4 − 0) = +3.75 m/s². Displacement = total area under the line (split into trapezium, rectangle, triangle).

Feature of the v–t graphWhat it tells you
Gradient (slope)Acceleration — steeper = larger acceleration
Area between the line and the t-axisDisplacement over that time interval
Horizontal line above the axisConstant velocity in the positive direction
Horizontal line on the axisAt rest (v = 0)
Line below the axisMoving in the negative direction
Crossing the axisChanging direction — momentary stop at the crossing

The single biggest confusion in this unit

On a position–time graph only the gradient is meaningful for motion (velocity); the area under a p–t graph has no physical use. On a velocity–time graph both are meaningful: gradient = acceleration, area = displacement. Students lose marks every year by computing the area under a p–t graph — check the axis labels before you calculate anything.

Worked example — two-stage rocket, read both features

Task: A model rocket's velocity–time graph rises linearly from 0 to 50 m/s in 5 s, stays constant at 50 m/s until t = 12 s, then falls linearly back to 0 at t = 20 s. Describe the motion and find the total displacement.

Stage 1 (0–5 s): positive constant gradient → a = 50/5 = +10 m/s², speeding up upwards. Displacement = ½ × 5 × 50 = 125 m.

Stage 2 (5–12 s): zero gradient → a = 0, constant velocity 50 m/s. Displacement = 7 × 50 = 350 m.

Stage 3 (12–20 s): negative gradient → a = (0 − 50)/8 = −6.25 m/s², still moving up but slowing. Displacement = ½ × 8 × 50 = 200 m.

Total displacement: 125 + 350 + 200 = 675 m above the launch pad. Note the rocket never goes down in this window — velocity stays positive throughout, so it keeps climbing even while slowing.

Interactive set · Self-check 5

On a velocity–time graph, which feature gives the object's acceleration?

A. The height of the line above the time axis
B. The area between the line and the time axis
C. The gradient (steepness) of the line
D. The point where the line crosses the vertical axis
Gradient = acceleration on a v–t graph (units: (m/s)/s = m/s²). Height gives the velocity value itself; area gives displacement. Mixing these two up is the classic v–t error.
Interactive set · Self-check 6

A velocity–time graph shows a line that starts at 0, rises to +8 m/s at 4 s, then falls back to 0 at 10 s (always above the axis). The object:

A. Returns to its starting point at 10 s
B. Keeps moving in the same direction the whole time, ending far from the start
C. Changes direction at 4 s
D. Is at rest between 4 s and 10 s
The velocity never becomes negative — the object always moves forward, first faster then slower. Area keeps accumulating, so displacement only grows. A change of direction requires the line to cross the t-axis.
Interactive set · Self-check 7

The slope of a position–time graph is negative and becoming less steep (flatter). The object is:

A. Speeding up in the positive direction
B. Slowing down in the positive direction
C. Speeding up in the negative direction
D. Slowing down in the negative direction
Negative slope → velocity is negative. Flatter → the slope's magnitude shrinks → the speed decreases towards zero. So the object moves in the negative direction and slows down: v is negative while a is positive (opposite signs).
TOPIC GROUP 04 · MOTION FORMULAE

Speed, Velocity, Acceleration and the SUVAT Equations

The three defining equations

QuantityDefinitionEquationUnits
SpeedDistance travelled per unit time (scalar)speed = distance / timem/s
VelocityRate of change of position (vector)v = Δx / Δt = (xf − xi) / tm/s
AccelerationRate of change of velocity (vector)a = Δv / Δt = (v − u) / tm/s²

Here u = initial velocity, v = final velocity, a = acceleration, t = time, s = displacement. These five symbols are the inputs and outputs of every SUVAT equation.

The four SUVAT equations

These apply only when acceleration is constant (uniform) and motion is in a straight line — exactly the situation in every M10 mechanics question:

v = u + atNo s — use when displacement is unknown or unwanted.
s = ½(u + v)tNo a — use when acceleration is unknown; average velocity × time.
s = ut + ½at²No v — use when final velocity is unknown.
v² = u² + 2asNo t — use when time is unknown.

Problem-solving strategy (use it every time)

1. Draw a number line; mark + direction. 2. List every symbol you know with its sign and unit (convert to m, m/s, m/s² first!). 3. Write down what you must find. 4. Choose the equation that contains your unknown and only knowns. 5. Substitute with units; calculate; 6. sanity-check the sign and size.

Unit conversions you must be fluent in

  • km/h → m/s: divide by 3.6. (110 km/h on the E4 → 110/3.6 ≈ 31 m/s.)
  • m/s → km/h: multiply by 3.6.
  • cm/s² → m/s²: divide by 100 (96 cm/s² = 0.96 m/s²).
  • SI prefixes: nano = 10−9, micro = 10−6, milli = 10−3, centi = 10−2, kilo = 10³, mega = 10&sup6;, giga = 10&sup9;.
  • g on Earth: the MYP data sheet gives g = 10 m/s² (rounded). Some handouts use the more precise 9.81 N/kg — whichever value a question states is the one you must use, and state your choice in your working.
Worked example 1 — acceleration from a roller-coaster drop

Task: Sophia (m = 52 kg) experiences a net force of 1800 N at the bottom of a loop. First find her acceleration (forces) — then, as a kinematics check, if her velocity there changes from 8 m/s to 19 m/s in 3.1 s, what acceleration does the kinematics give?

Kinematics route: a = Δv/t = (19 − 8)/3.1 = 11/3.1 = 3.5 m/s² (to 2 s.f.).

Forces route (preview of Section 5): a = Fnet/m = 1800/52 = 34.6 ≈ 35 m/s² — a much larger instant because the loop's bottom is a brief, violent phase; over the longer 3.1 s window the average is smaller. Both routes are valid; the question tells you which numbers describe which moment.

Worked example 2 — free fall with g = 10 m/s²

Task: A ball is dropped from rest from a bridge 45 m above water. Ignoring air resistance, how long does it take to hit the water, and at what speed?

Knowns: u = 0 m/s, s = 45 m, a = +10 m/s² (down taken as positive), t = ? and v = ?

Find t: use s = ut + ½at² → 45 = 0 + ½ × 10 × t² → t² = 9 → t = 3.0 s.

Find v: use v = u + at = 0 + 10 × 3.0 = 30 m/s (or v² = u² + 2as = 0 + 2×10×45 = 900 → v = 30 m/s — same answer, good check).

Interactive set · Self-check 8

A car accelerates uniformly from rest at 3 m/s² for 6 s. Its final velocity is:

A. 0.5 m/s
B. 18 m/s
C. 54 m/s
D. 108 m/s
v = u + at = 0 + 3 × 6 = 18 m/s. Option C (54) is s = ut + ½at² = 54 m — the displacement, not the velocity. Option A inverts the equation. Always check units: m/s² × s = m/s.
Interactive set · Self-check 9

Which SUVAT equation should you use when you know u, a and s, and must find v (time unknown)?

A. v² = u² + 2as
B. v = u + at
C. s = ut + ½at²
D. s = ½(u + v)t
Eliminate any equation containing the unknown t. B, C and D all contain t; only A links u, v, a and s directly. This elimination method is faster than memorising "when to use which" tables.

Do not confuse

Speed vs velocity. Speed is how fast (scalar). Velocity is how fast and which way (vector). A car that completes a 1 km lap at 20 m/s average speed has average velocity of zero — it ended where it started. Also: "average speed" = total distance / total time, while "average velocity" = total displacement / total time. They only coincide when the motion never reverses.

TOPIC GROUP 05 · FORCES

Balanced and Unbalanced Forces — Newton's Laws

What a force is

A force is a push or pull resulting from an interaction between two objects. Forces are measured in newtons (N) and are vectors. Forces never exist in isolation: every force named in a problem has an agent (what exerts it) and a receiver (what feels it).

Newton's three laws in one table

LawStatementHow questions test it
1stAn object keeps its velocity (including rest, v = 0) unless a net (unbalanced) force acts on it.Given constant velocity → forces are balanced. Given changing velocity → unbalanced.
2ndFnet = ma — acceleration is in the same direction as the net force and inversely proportional to mass.Calculate any one of F, m, a from the other two; handle proportionality ("double F, same m → double a").
3rdEvery force has an equal and opposite partner acting on the other object.Identify N3 pairs (book on table ↔ table on book). Traps: pairs never act on the same object, so they never cancel each other.

Weight — the gravitational force

Fg = mg gives the gravitational force (weight) on an object of mass m in a field of strength g. On Earth's surface g ≈ 9.81 N/kg (the MYP data sheet rounds this to 10 m/s² for calculation). "Weight" is simply another name for this force — it is not the same quantity as mass.

MassWeight
What it isAmount of matter in an objectGravitational force pulling on that matter
TypeScalarVector (always towards the planet's centre)
UnitkgN
Changes with location?No — same on Earth, Moon, in spaceYes — scales with local g (Moon g ≈ 1.6 N/kg)
Measured byBalance (beam or electronic)Spring scale / newton meter

Free-body diagrams (FBDs)

A free-body diagram shows one object only, with arrows for every force acting on it. Arrow length must be proportional to magnitude. At M10 we only consider forces that are purely vertical or purely horizontal — diagonal components come next year.

Book at rest on a table — balanced

The upward force is the table's support (normal force). Equal length arrows: net force zero, so the book stays at rest (Newton's 1st law).

Wagon pulled forward — unbalanced

Horizontal arrows differ in length → net force 28 N forward → acceleration forward. (Vertical weight and support forces cancel and are often omitted once you have confirmed they balance.)

Finding the net force from an FBD

Add all forces in the positive direction, subtract all in the negative direction. If the result is zero → balanced. If not → the net force points in the sign of the result, and then a = Fnet/m gives both the size and direction of the acceleration.

Worked example 1 — weight of an African elephant

Task: An African elephant has mass 6000 kg. Find its weight on Earth in newtons, using g = 10 m/s² (data-sheet value).

Fg = mg = 6000 × 10 = 60 000 N (6.0 × 10&sup4; N to 2 s.f.). With g = 9.81 it would be 58 860 N ≈ 5.9 × 10&sup4; N. The mass is unchanged on the Moon; the weight there would be only about 6000 × 1.6 ≈ 9600 N.

Worked example 2 — full FBD chain (suitcase problem)

Task: Vanessa pulls a stuck suitcase. Vertical forces: Fgrav = Fnorm = 207 N (balanced). Horizontal: tension Ftens = 182 N forwards, kinetic friction Ffrict = 166 N backwards. Find the net force, the mass and the acceleration.

Step 1 — net force: Fnet = 182 − 166 = 16 N, forwards.

Step 2 — mass: weight = mg → m = 207/10 = 20.7 kg (using data-sheet g = 10; with 9.81: 21.1 kg).

Step 3 — acceleration: a = Fnet/m = 16/20.7 = 0.77 m/s², forwards (0.76 with g = 9.81). Same direction as the net force — always.

Worked example 3 — Newton's 3rd law pair hunt

Task: Identify every Newton's 3rd law pair in the elephant-on-floor situation, and explain why the elephant's weight and the floor's support force are not a pair.

Pairs (act on different objects, same interaction): (1) Earth pulls elephant down ↔ elephant pulls Earth up. (2) Elephant pushes floor down ↔ floor pushes elephant up (the normal/support force).

Not a pair: Fg (Earth↔elephant) and the support force (floor↔elephant) both act on the elephant, so they cannot be a 3rd-law pair. They happen to be equal in size because the elephant is not accelerating vertically — that is Newton's 1st/2nd law balance, not the 3rd law. Students who confuse these two ideas claim the forces "cancel as a pair"; they cancel because the net force is zero, not because they are partners.

Interactive set · Self-check 10

A box sits on a rough floor and a horizontal push of 30 N exactly equals the friction force. The box:

A. Must accelerate in the direction of the push
B. Is in equilibrium only if it is already moving
C. Has zero net force, so it stays at rest or continues at constant velocity
D. Experiences forces that violate Newton's 3rd law
Equal horizontal forces → Fₓet = 0 → balanced. Newton's 1st law: velocity does not change — whether that is rest or constant motion depends on what happened before, not on the forces now.
Interactive set · Self-check 11

A 4 kg cart accelerates at 3 m/s². The net force on it is:

A. 0.75 N
B. 12 N
C. 7 N
D. 40 N
Fₓet = ma = 4 × 3 = 12 N. Option C (7) adds the numbers instead of multiplying — forces multiply mass and acceleration, they never add them. Option D wrongly uses g = 10 as the acceleration.
Interactive set · Self-check 12

A skydiver of mass 82.5 kg experiences 118 N of air resistance while falling. Taking down as positive and g = 10 m/s², the acceleration is:

A. 1.43 m/s² down
B. 10 m/s² down
C. 8.57 m/s² up
D. 8.6 m/s² down
Weight = 82.5 × 10 = 825 N down; air resistance 118 N up; Fₓet = 825 − 118 = 707 N down. a = 707/82.5 ≈ 8.6 m/s² down. Always draw the FBD before dividing — the air resistance reduces the acceleration below g, it does not reverse it.

Do not confuse

3rd-law pairs vs balanced forces. A 3rd-law pair always involves two different objects (A on B, B on A) — such a pair can never be "cancelled", because they act on different bodies. Balanced forces act on the same object and sum to zero. A question that asks "the weight of the book and the support of the table are an action-reaction pair — discuss" is fishing for exactly this distinction.

Mechanics checklist — the fastest way to score

For any graph question: check the axis labels first, then decide gradient or area. For any force question: draw the FBD first, then sum forces, then F = ma. For any SUVAT question: list u, v, a, s, t with signs and units before choosing an equation. Marks are awarded for the method as well as the number.
