Biodiversity

How many organisms share the planet?

Estimates vary enormously — from about 8 million to 20 million eukaryotic species. The truth is almost certainly higher, because most species are still undescribed.

EstimateMethodWhy it may be wrong
~8.7 millionStatistical extrapolation from samples of soil/canopyIgnores deep ocean, fungi, parasites
~20 millionRatio of undescribed to described species in well-studied taxaAssumes all taxa behave like beetles
~100 millionIncludes prokaryotes, viruses, unknown bacteriaSpecies concept becomes problematic
8.7 million – 20 millionCurrent consensus (Mora et al. 2011)Still an estimate, not a count

Why estimates vary so widely — 5 reasons to learn

  • Undersampling — most habitats (deep sea, tropical soil, canopy) have barely been surveyed.
  • The species problem — different species definitions give different counts, especially for bacteria and fungi.
  • Cryptic species — morphologically identical organisms that are genetically distinct.
  • Which groups are counted — including bacteria and viruses massively increases the total.
  • Taxonomic effort is unequal — vertebrates are studied; nematodes are not.

Species and the binomial naming system

A species is a group of organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring. Under the Biological Species Concept, members of the same species share a gene pool and are reproductively isolated from other species.

Limitations of the biological species concept

  • Fossils and asexual organisms (bacteria, many fungi) cannot be tested.
  • Some species can interbreed (horse × donkey) but are still separate species.
  • Geographically isolated populations may never meet to test interbreeding.

Every species is given a unique binomial name of two parts: Genus (capitalised) + specific epithet (lowercase), both in italics.

What is biodiversity?

Biodiversity = the variety of all living organisms on Earth. It has three measurable levels:

  • Genetic diversity — variation within a species (the raw material for evolution).
  • Species diversity — the variety of species in a habitat; measured as species richness (number of species) and evenness (how equally individuals are distributed among them).
  • Ecosystem diversity — the variety of habitats and ecological roles.

Regional patterns of biodiversity

  • Latitudinal gradient — richness increases from the poles toward the equator. Equatorial rainforests hold a disproportionate share of species.
  • Two main hypotheses: energy/light availability (more solar energy → more photosynthesis → more niches) and glacial stability (equatorial regions were never buried under ice, so lineages had more time to diversify).
  • Hotspots — small areas with exceptional endemism: the Tropical Andes, Madagascar, the Mediterranean Basin, Southeast Asia, the Caribbean.
  • Coral reefs — under 1% of the ocean floor but about 25% of marine species.
  • Biodiversity is not evenly distributed, and neither is the threat to it — hotspots overlap heavily with high human pressure.

Purpose and structure — exam-worthy

  • Purpose: to identify which species need urgent conservation action, and to inform and prioritise policy, funding and protected-area planning.
  • Structure: every species is assessed against nine quantitative criteria (population size, decline rate, geographic range, habitat quality, exploitation, and so on) plus sub-criteria.
  • Categories are assigned on a risk of extinction scale, from EX down to LC. "Threatened" collectively means CR + EN + VU.
  • Assessments are reviewed and updated regularly, so a category can improve (species recover) or worsen.

Conservation measures and why conserve?

In situProtecting species in their natural habitat — national parks, wildlife reserves, marine protected areas. Highest effectiveness.
Ex situRemoving organisms to protect them — zoos, seed banks, botanical gardens, cryopreservation.
Captive breedingBreeding endangered species in controlled conditions, e.g. the Arabian oryx and the California condor.
Habitat restorationReplanting degraded ecosystems, e.g. restoring kelp forests or Swedish wetland mosaics.
Anti-poaching & trade controlsCITES agreements restrict international trade in endangered species and their parts.
Protected areasCurrently about 17% of land and 8% of ocean is protected — many target the wrong places.

Why conserve species? Ecosystem services (clean air, water purification, pollination, soil formation, food and medicine), scientific value (each species is a library of genetic information and a line of evidence), economic value (fisheries, tourism, jobs), aesthetic and ethical value, and the intrinsic value of life itself.

Different perspectives

Conservationist view

Every species has intrinsic value and an right to exist. Extinction is irreversible and morally unacceptable. We should accept costs now to prevent permanent loss.

Development / utilitarian view

Resources should be used to benefit present human populations. Some species can be sacrificed if the economic gain is large enough — though extinction risks a "use value" loss too.

Other positions: an ecocentric view (the ecosystem, not individual species, has value), a deep ecology view (intrinsic value of all life), and a stewardship view (we are caretakers for future generations). Evaluating these is Criterion D work.

Origin of Life and the Development of Life on Earth

How is Earth history divided?

Geological time is organised in a nested hierarchy based on the fossil record and plate tectonics.

Interpreting a geological timeline — the three questions

  1. Which eon/era/period does this event fall in? Remember Era > Period > Epoch > Age, and Eon > Era.
  2. Is the boundary a sudden event or gradual? Sharp boundaries usually mark mass extinctions; gradual boundaries mark climate or tectonic change.
  3. What is the absolute age? Eon, era and period are relative units; radioisotope dating (uranium-lead, potassium-argon, carbon-14) gives absolute ages. Note the nearly logarithmic scale: recent time is hugely compressed.

Conditions on the early Earth

  • No free oxygen. The early atmosphere was rich in CO₂, water vapour, methane (CH₄) and ammonia (NH₃) — the reducing atmosphere. Today's 21% oxygen only arrived after photosynthesis.
  • Heavy bombardment from asteroids and comets, plus continuous volcanic activity and frequent large impacts.
  • Conditions suitable for prebiotic chemistry — UV radiation, lightning and hydrothermal vents as possible energy sources for building organic molecules.
  • No ozone layer, so UV radiation reached the surface directly.
  • Once the Earth cooled, water vapour condensed into oceans ~3.8 billion years ago. Water was essential: it was the solvent in which organic chemistry happened.

The origin of life

Life required a self-replicating molecule capable of variation. The RNA world hypothesis is the leading proposal: RNA can both store information (like DNA) and catalyse reactions (like enzymes), so a single molecule could in principle do both jobs.

  • 1. Simple organic molecules formMiller-Urey experiment (1953) — sparks passed through a mixture of methane, ammonia, hydrogen and water produced amino acids. Confirms abiotic synthesis of organic compounds is possible.
  • 2. Complex molecules / protocellsMacromolecules assemble — amino acids into proteins, sugars, nucleotides. Lipids self-assemble into liposomes that can divide, offering a simple boundary for a protocell.
  • 3. Self-replication and variationRNA world — a self-replicating RNA molecule with occasional copying errors. Natural selection begins the moment imperfect replication appears.
  • 4. First true cellsProkaryotic cells ~3.5–3.8 bya, with a cell membrane but no nucleus. Evidence: stromatolites and microfossils in Pilbara, Western Australia and Greenland.
  • 5. EndosymbiosisMitochondria then chloroplasts — aerobic prokaryotes engulfed by others and became permanent organelles, giving eukaryotic cells.
  • 6. ComplexityMulticellularity, then sex, then the Cambrian explosion — the rapid diversification of animal body plans ~541 Ma.

Alternative energy sources for the first life

  • Photosynthesis — used by cyanobacteria from ~2.7 bya; needs light, so limited to the photic zone.
  • Chemosynthesis — organisms obtain energy from the oxidation of inorganic chemicals (H₂S, NH₃, Fe²⁺, H₂) instead of light. Supports entire ecosystems with no sunlight: hydrothermal vent communities, deep-sea vents, and Sweden's excavated lake beds (e.g. hydrothermal mining areas such as Garpenberg) where chemosynthesis by microbes is the base of the food web.

LUCA — the Last Universal Common Ancestor

Before there were separate species there was a single ancestral population. LUCA is the hypothetical population of the last common ancestor of all living organisms, thought to have lived roughly 3.8–4.0 billion years ago.

What scientists think LUCA was like

  • A single-celled, prokaryote-like organism — no nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles.
  • Heterotrophic (obtained energy from organic molecules) or a simple chemoautotroph.
  • Possibly living at a hydrothermal vent, where a natural proton gradient could power early ATP production — the natural chemiosmotic hypothesis.
  • Had a cell membrane, DNA, RNA, ribosomes and a working metabolic pathway.

How do we know anything about LUCA and the origin of life?

EvidenceWhat it tells us
Phylogenetic analysis of genesAll organisms share homologous genes (rRNA, cytochrome c) → descent from a common ancestor
Universal genetic codeNear-identical codon assignments across all life
Earliest fossils & stromatolitesLife existed by ~3.5–3.8 bya — surprisingly fast after Earth formed at 4.6 bya
Isotopic signaturesCarbon isotope ratios in ancient rocks indicate biological carbon fixation
Miller-Urey & modern chemistryAmino acids and other organics can form abiotically
Genes in "primitive" organismsThermophiles and archaea retain ancient metabolism

Exam tip

LUCA is a hypothesis inferred from present life, not a fossil. Any answer claiming we have found LUCA's fossils is incorrect. The honest answer is that we infer it by comparing the genomes of living organisms and tracing them back to their deepest shared ancestor.

Evidence that all life on Earth is related

  • Common DNA and a universal genetic code — the strongest evidence. All organisms use the same 4 bases and largely the same codon meanings.
  • Homologous structures — the same bone plan in a human arm, a bat wing and a whale flipper.
  • Shared biochemistry — every cell uses the same ATP, the same 20 amino acids, the same ribosomes, cell membrane structure.
  • Nested fossil sequence — new groups appear in the rock record in the correct order and are never found in older layers.
  • Phylogenetic trees — organisms group together by genetic similarity exactly as morphology predicts.
  • Biogeography — island species resemble those of the nearest mainland, but have diverged from them.

Mass extinction events

A mass extinction is a short geologically-speaking event in which a large proportion (often >75%) of species disappear, and which shows up as a sharp unconformity in the rock record.

EventWhenSpecies lostMain cause
Great Oxidation~2.4 byaMany anaerobic prokaryotesAccumulation of toxic oxygen

Late Devonian~372 Ma~75% speciesOcean anoxia; widespread plant death and nutrient runoff
End-Permian252 Ma~96% — the largestSiberian Trapp volcanism → CO₂ spike, ocean warming, acidification, anoxia

The end-Cretaceous event in detail

66 million years ago, ~75% of species including all non-avian dinosaurs were wiped out. Evidence used to establish the cause:

  • The Iridium anomaly — Luis and Walter Alvarez found an unusually high iridium concentration in a thin clay layer at Gubbio, Italy, and at the K-Pg boundary worldwide. Iridium is common in asteroids but extremely rare in Earth's crust. This was the smoking gun.
  • The Chicxulub Crater — a 180 km impact crater under the Yucatén Peninsula, dated to exactly 66 Ma, with a shocked-quartz layer and tektite (Cretaceous impact glass) deposits worldwide.
  • The K-Pg boundary layer — a worldwide thin layer enriched in iridium and shocked quartz, marking the event precisely.
  • Extinction pattern — selective: large-bodied, slow-reproducing, non-burrowing and plant-dependent species died out, while small burrowing and generalist species survived. This matches the sudden darkness and food-web collapse an impact would cause, rather than a gradual climate-driven extinction.
  • Deccan Trapp volcanism — massive basalt volcanism in India coincides with the same interval, so volcanism probably contributed a lethal heat and CO₂ burden.

Causes of mass extinctions — general categories

  • Asteroid / comet impact — end-Cretaceous.
  • Mass volcanism — end-Permian (CO₂ and anoxia), end-Triassic.
  • Continental drift & climate change — altered ocean circulation, glaciations, loss of shallow-shelf habitat.
  • Anoxia — warming reduces oxygen solubility; stagnant water becomes anoxic.
  • Sea-level change — marine regressions drain shallow seas that hosted most marine species.
  • Existing vulnerability — small range, low population, narrow diet, poor dispersal.

Broad patterns of evolution over time

  • Increasing complexity — but not a steady ladder; complexity can be lost (e.g. many parasites have simplified).
  • Increasing body size in many lineages (Cope's rule) early on, followed by the frequent evolution of much smaller organisms.
  • Mass extinctions are always followed by recovery and adaptive radiation, but the recovering groups are different from those lost (e.g. dinosaurs → mammals).
  • Biodiversity rises overall over 3.8 billion years, interrupted by five sharp drops.
  • Speciation is usually rapid (geologically), and most species have a short geological lifespan — the "light bulb" model of stasis punctuated by change.

Measuring Populations and Biodiversity

Species richness and other measures

  • Species richness (S) — the number of different species present in a given area. Simple, but ignores evenness.
  • Species diversity (&e.g. S and E, or the Simpson index) — richness combined with how evenly individuals are spread across species.
  • Abundance — number of individuals of one species in an area.
  • Percentage cover — the proportion of ground occupied by a species, especially for plants and sessile organisms.
  • Biomass — total mass of organisms, useful for comparing communities with different species sizes.

Why richness alone is not enough

A community with 4 species and 25 individuals each is more diverse than one with 4 species where one species has 97 individuals and the others have 1 each. Both have S = 4, but the first has far higher evenness and far greater resilience, because no single species dominates.

Random sampling

Random sampling selects every point with equal probability, giving every part of the area an equal chance of being chosen. This avoids selection bias, where the researcher's convenience (sampling the path, the sunny edge) distorts the data.

  • Systematic sampling — a random start point, then a fixed interval (every 2 m along a transect). Often preferred in practice: still unbiased if the start is random, and far faster.
  • Stratified sampling — divide the area into strata and sample each proportionally. Best where the area is clearly heterogeneous (e.g. sunlit vs shaded).
  • Opportunity sampling — non-random, only convenient spots. Acceptable only for a preliminary survey — it can never be used to estimate population size.
  • Repeat for a large number of quadrats — a minimum of about 20, spread across the whole area, is expected to produce a reliable mean.

Field studies: quadrats, transects and the calculation

  1. Mark out the study area with a frame and map it.
  2. Place a quadrat (typically 50 cm × 50 cm, or 1 m × 1 m) at a randomly chosen point.
  3. Count / identify each organism inside. Plants: count or estimate % cover. Animals: count, or use capture-mark-recapture.
  4. Repeat many times across the area.
  5. Calculate the mean per quadrat, then scale up to the whole area.

The working

Area of one quadrat = 0.5 m × 0.5 m = 0.25 m². Mean dandelions per quadrat = 8. Total study area = 200 m².

Number of quadrats in 200 m² = 200 ÷ 0.25 = 800.
Estimated dandelions = 8 × 800 = 6,400 plants.

Capture-mark-recapture (for mobile animals)

  • Catch and mark (harmless mark, or a non-invasive tag) and release a sample of M.
  • Allow time to mix.
  • Catch a second sample of C; count the marked individuals R already tagged.
  • Estimated population N = (M × C) ÷ R

Worked example: 40 frogs marked and released; later 30 caught, of which 10 were marked. N = (40 × 30) ÷ 10 = 120 frogs.

Assumptions and limitations of fieldwork

  • Marking must be non-harmful and must not change behaviour or survival.
  • The sample must be random and the population closed (no births, deaths, immigration or emigration between samples).
  • Animals must mix completely and evenly, and marks must persist and not be lost.
  • Random error is reduced by increasing the number of quadrats — but the mean converges only as roughly the square root of the sample size, so accuracy improves slowly.
  • Systematic error (choosing a poor quadrat size, placing quadrats in a path) is not fixed by repeating more.
  • Small, mobile or hidden organisms (birds, burrowing animals, deep-water species) are poorly sampled by quadrats.

Analysing fieldwork data

  • Organise raw counts in a table: quadrat number, species A, species B, total, then mean and standard deviation per species.
  • Compare a range to decide if the mean is representative. A large range means high variability — the population is patchy, or the sampling is inadequate.
  • Check for outliers. One anomalous quadrat (someone stood on a plant) distorts the mean; consider excluding it and stating why.
  • Present with the error bars / ±1 SD and sample size — a mean without n is incomplete evidence.

Evolution

The VIDA model of evolution by natural selection

Evolution is not a goal-driven process. VIDA summarises the four requirements, and all four must hold together for evolution to occur by natural selection.

V
VariationIndividuals in a population differ in their characteristics.
I
InheritanceThose differences are passed on as heritable traits by genes.
D
Differential survivalIndividuals with certain traits survive and reproduce more successfully.
A
AdaptationOver generations the favourable traits become common in the population.

The three conditions of natural selection

  • Overproduction of offspring — more offspring are produced than can survive, so there is competition for limited resources.
  • Inherited variation — offspring differ because of random mutation and recombination, and the differences are heritable.
  • Differential survival and reproduction — some variants survive to reproduce more. The population's allele frequency shifts toward the advantageous traits.

Three classic exam traps

  1. Evolution is not "purposeful" — no organism evolves because it needs to; advantageous variation arises by random mutation, and selection filters it.
  2. Natural selection acts on the whole phenotype, but only the heritable part of the difference is passed on. Acquired traits (a tan, a muscle) are not inherited.
  3. Selection cannot invent a new variation — it can only sort existing variation. This is why mutation is the ultimate source of all genetic variation, and why populations with no variation cannot adapt.

How new species arise

  • Different populations become geographically isolated (a new river, mountain, glacier, or ocean channel forms).
  • Gene flow stops — no exchange of alleles.
  • Different selective pressures in each area, plus genetic drift and mutation, drive the populations apart.
  • Over long periods they become so different that they can no longer interbreed — reproductively isolated — so they are now separate species.

Isolating mechanisms that complete speciation: behavioural (different mating calls), mechanical (incompatible genitalia), gametic (sperm and egg won't fuse), temporal (different breeding seasons).

Divergent vs convergent evolution

Divergent — homologous

Definition: related species evolve apart from a shared ancestor, and structures become modified for different functions but keep the same underlying bone plan.

Evidence type: homologous structures — same origin, different function.

Example: the pentadactyl limb — human hand, cat paw, whale flipper, bat wing, all built from the same humerus / radius / ulna / carpals / metacarpals / phalanges, with different proportions and functions.

Convergent — analogous

Definition: distantly related species independently evolve similar features because they face the same environmental pressure. The structures are not inherited from a common ancestor with that feature.

Evidence type: analogous structures — same function, different origin.

Example: wings in birds, insects and bats; the streamlined shape of a shark, a dolphin (mammal) and an ichthyosaur; the camera eye of humans and octopuses.

How to tell them apart in an exam

Ask: "Does the structure come from a shared ancestor?" Same bone plan or same developmental pattern → homologous (divergent). Same function, different structure and origin → analogous (convergent). The classic trap is a dolphin flipper vs a shark fin: same function, but the dolphin's is a homologous forelimb with the same bones as a human arm.

Phylogenetic (evolutionary) trees

  • Nodes represent the most recent common ancestor of the branches above them.
  • Branch length usually represents time or amount of genetic change — check which the axis says.
  • Organisms that join at the most recent node are the most closely related, regardless of how far apart they are drawn on the page.
  • A group whose branches all cluster together is a clade (monophyletic group).
  • Read the tree upright: lineage order runs from the root (oldest) to the tips (most recent).

The fossil record as evidence

Fossils show that organisms have changed over time, that change is gradual and branching, and that the sequence matches the order predicted by the rock layers.

Limitations of the fossil record

  • Incomplete — only a tiny fraction of organisms fossilise, and those are mostly hard-bodied, marine or large.
  • Biased — we have far more fossils of vertebrates, dinosaurs and trilobites than of soft-bodied, small or tropical organisms.
  • Requires specific conditions — rapid burial, no scavengers, mineralisation. Fossils are therefore rare and localised.
  • Not an exact record — "missing" fossil gaps are real; we do not have a fossil for every species.
  • Can be misinterpreted — without DNA or other evidence, fossils cannot give absolute dates or prove evolutionary relationships on their own.
  • Dating is difficult and destructive — radiometric dating requires the right isotope and enough sample.

How the modern classification system shows relationships

Modern taxonomy is phylogenetic. The system is built on shared derived characteristics and on molecular (DNA/RNA) evidence rather than on overall similarity.

  • Moving from Domain → Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species, you are increasingly grouping organisms with a more recent common ancestor.
  • Analogous structures are ignored; homologous structures are treated as evidence.
  • Asymmetrical or coiled DNA and RNA sequences are the strongest evidence for relatedness.
  • Whales are classified as mammals, not fish, despite looking like fish — because of their skull, ear bones, jaw, forelimbs and DNA.
  • Kingdoms are monophyletic clades: each contains a common ancestor and all its descendants.

Galápagos Finches — Data Analysis

The case study

On the Galápagos Islands, Geospiza finches have diversified from a single common ancestor into several species. Darwin noticed that beaks differed between islands, and that the beak shape matched the food available on each island. Peter and Rosemary Grant then measured this over decades.

Ground finchLarge, deep, blunt beak — crushing hard seeds. Beak depth is genetically determined.
Cactus finchLong, pointed, curved beak — probing cactus flowers and fruit.
Warbler finchThin, pointed beak — catching insects. Uses a twig as a tool.
Sharp-beaked ground finchUsed as a tool to prise insects from crevices — tool use, like a woodpecker finch.
Large tree finchVery large beak for large, hard seeds — hybridisation has produced some separate species.
VegetationSeed size and hardness vary by island: small soft seeds on the wetter islands, large hard seeds on the dry ones.

The famous drought of 1976–77

Severe drought cut the food supply. Finches with deeper beaks could crack the few remaining hard seeds and therefore survived better. Over the drought, mean beak depth increased in the population. When the rains returned, the population's mean beak depth decreased again. This is natural selection observed directly — and a reminder that evolution can also go backwards as pressures change.

Choosing the right chart

ChartUse it forIn this case study

Bar chart vs histogram — do not confuse them

A bar chart has gaps between bars because the categories are separate and the order is arbitrary. A histogram has touching bars because it is one continuous variable split into bins; the area of each bar equals its frequency. Plotting a histogram of species (discrete) instead of beak depth (continuous) is a data-handling error.

Mean and standard deviation

You must be able to interpret these; you do not need to calculate standard deviation by hand.

  • Mean (x̄) — the average; the balance point of the data. Sensitive to outliers.
  • Standard deviation (s) — the typical distance of values from the mean. A measure of spread, not of the size of the mean.
  • Small SD → tightly clustered, low variation, a stable population (or suspiciously uniform data).
  • Large SD → wide variation, greater potential for the population to respond to a change in conditions — and a higher chance of an outlier distorting the mean.
  • Always quote n (sample size) alongside the mean, and use ±1 SD error bars on a chart.

Worked interpretation

Ground finches on Genovesa: mean beak depth 11.2 mm, SD 1.1 mm, n = 78.
Ground finches on Daphne Major: mean beak depth 12.4 mm, SD 0.9 mm, n = 92.

Conclusion: Daphne Major birds have a larger mean (deeper beaks, so better adapted to hard seeds) but less variation. So Daphne Major is a harder environment — only the best-adapted individuals survive — whereas Genovesa is easier, so more phenotypes persist. Genovesa's population is more resilient to a sudden change because it holds more genetic variety.

Evaluating the data and the method

Criterion D work always asks you to judge the evidence, not just quote it.

  • Limitations of using beak morphology as the only variable: it ignores behaviour, which is at least as important. Darwin himself underappreciated song and courtship behaviour in finch speciation.
  • Worshipping morphology has caused errors — some historical finch "species" (e.g. G. conirostris) turned out to be hybrids or subspecies once DNA was compared.
  • Biased samples: the small, widely scattered Galápagos population, birds caught in mist nets (which may be sex- or size-biased), and different sampling effort across islands.
  • Beak depth is highly heritable, but beak length is much less so — so not all measured traits respond to selection equally.
  • Long-term data is rare and valuable — the Grants' 40-year record is a strength, but the drought years are an exceptional case, so generalising from them is risky.
  • Correlation is not causation. Beak size correlates with seed size here, but you need evidence that the birds did not simply prefer the same foods the seed abundance made available.
TOPIC GROUP 06 · CLASS 9

Mutations and Human Evolution

What is a mutation?

A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence of a gene or chromosome. It is the ultimate source of all genetic variation — without mutation, natural selection has nothing to act on and populations would eventually run out of variation.

  • Mutagens are agents that increase the mutation rate: ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays, ultraviolet light), chemical mutagens (tobacco smoke, asbestos, mustard gas, some pesticides), and biological agents (some viruses).
  • Spontaneous mutations occur naturally from DNA copying errors and from natural background radiation, with no mutagen needed.
  • Most mutations are neutral, some are harmful, and very few are beneficial.

Types of mutation

TypeScaleExample
Point / substitutionOne base pairSickle cell: one base change turns glutamic acid into valine in haemoglobin
Insertion / deletion (indels)One or more base pairsCystic fibrosis: deletion of 3 bases in the CFTR gene
ChromosomalWhole chromosomes / setsDown syndrome (trisomy 21), Turner's syndrome (XO)
DuplicationGene copiesGlobin gene duplications → different haemoglobin chains

How mutations drive evolution

Mutations are random with respect to need. They are not produced because an organism requires them, but because the environment is full of mutagens and DNA copying is imperfect.

  • A mutation creates a new allele in one individual.
  • If it is invisible (recessive, or in a non-coding region) it may drift around harmlessly for many generations.
  • If the environment creates a selective pressure that the new trait happens to overcome, carriers leave more offspring.
  • The allele's frequency increases in the population over generations — that is evolution by natural selection.

Key distinction for the exam

Mutation supplies the variation; natural selection sorts it. A mutation is not "useful" or "harmful" in itself — it is only described that way relative to the current environment. A trait that is disadvantageous at 20 °C may be advantageous in a hotter climate, which is exactly how recently evolved human variations are explained.

How selective pressures favour particular mutations

Natural selection favours a mutation when the organism carrying it has higher reproductive success in that particular environment — it survives longer, or finds more mates, or produces more viable offspring. This is called a selective pressure: an environmental factor that alters relative survival or reproduction.

Examples of selective pressures: temperature, altitude and UV intensity, diet, disease and pathogen load, salinity, and the availability of a particular food source.

Geographical patterns of recently evolved human traits

Because different regions impose different selective pressures, and because populations are largely isolated, different mutations become common in different places. These are called local adaptations.

1. Adult lactose tolerance

  • All mammals produce lactase as infants but normally stop producing it after weaning.
  • A single mutation in the LCT/MCM6 regulatory region keeps lactase production switched on into adulthood.
  • It is extremely rare in most populations — yet around 35–50% of adults in northern Europe are tolerant.
  • Selective pressure: cattle pastoralism. Domestication of cattle ~7,000 years ago in northern Europe, and ~9,000 years ago in East Africa (where the mutation is also very common, independently — convergent evolution).
  • Evidence: ancient DNA from Neolithic skeletons shows lactase persistence alleles rising sharply in frequency in the same populations that took up dairying — a very strong selective sweep.

2. Alcohol response

  • ALDH2*2 (a variant of aldehyde dehydrogenase) breaks down acetaldehyde. The common East Asian variant produces a much less effective enzyme, so acetaldehyde accumulates.
  • Carriers experience flushing, nausea and headaches after drinking, and consequently drink less and have lower rates of alcoholism.
  • Selective pressure hypothesis: historically rice fermentation produced alcoholic drinks; individuals who could not metabolise them were less likely to drink and more likely to survive — while H. pylori infection, which raises gastric cancer risk, is also lower in ALDH2*2 carriers.
  • Notable regional asymmetry: the allele that causes strong flushing is very common in East Asia but absent in most European and African populations, which instead show the high-alcohol-metabolising ADH1B variant.

3. Sickle cell trait — the classic case

  • A single base substitution in the beta-globin gene (HbA → HbS) changes glutamic acid to valine.
  • Heterozygous (HbAS) carriers have sickle cell trait: usually healthy, but under low oxygen — high altitude, exertion, dehydration — their red cells can sickle.
  • Homozygous (HbSS) individuals have sickle cell anaemia: severe and often fatal without treatment.
  • The selective pressure: Plasmodium falciparum malaria. HbAS carriers have markedly increased resistance to severe malaria, so they survive and reproduce more successfully in malarial regions.
  • The geographical pattern matches the malaria map almost exactly: high frequencies in sub-Saharan Africa, the Mediterranean, the Middle East, and India; rare or absent in northern Europe, East Asia and the Americas.
  • This is balanced (polymorphic) selection: the allele is harmful in homozygotes but beneficial in heterozygotes, so natural selection maintains it in the population rather than fixing or eliminating it.

Swedish / local example

Milk and lactose: Sweden has one of the highest adult lactose-tolerance rates in the world (often cited above 90%), together with the Netherlands, Germany and the UK — a direct cultural-historical legacy of Scandinavian dairy farming. Contrast this with East Asia, where adult lactose intolerance is the majority phenotype. The same environment (a dairy-rich diet) is not required; the difference is cultural and genetic.

Distinguishing human variation

  • Allele frequency data across many populations lets you infer which variants are recent (unevenly distributed) versus ancient (evenly distributed — meaning they predate the dispersal of modern humans from Africa, ~70,000 years ago).
  • Not all variation is adaptive. Much of it is genetic drift — random changes in allele frequency, strongest in small, isolated populations.
  • Race is not a valid biological category. Human genetic variation is continuous and overlapping; populations are clines, not discrete types, and most of the variation is within populations rather than between them.
